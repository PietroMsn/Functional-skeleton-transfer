\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
% ============================================================================

We build on the functional skeleton transfer of \citet{musoni2021functional}.
A rigged source shape $\M$ with $n_\M$ vertices $\X_\M \in \R^{n_\M \times 3}$,
skinning weights and a skeleton of $Q$ joints $\J_\M \in \R^{Q \times 3}$ with
parent map $p(\cdot)$ is given, together with an unrigged target $\N$ (a mesh or
a point cloud with vertices $\X_\N$) and a small set of corresponding landmarks.
We denote by $\Phi \in \R^{n_\M \times k_\M}$ and $\Psi \in \R^{n_\N \times k_\N}$
the first Laplace--Beltrami eigenfunctions of the two shapes, orthonormal with
respect to the lumped mass matrices $\mathbf{M}_\M$, $\mathbf{M}_\N$, so that
$\Phi^\dagger = \Phi^\top \mathbf{M}_\M$. The original pipeline
(i) fits a spatial joint regressor $\Rspat \in \R^{Q \times n_\M}$ on the
source, $\J_\M \approx \Rspat \X_\M$; (ii) estimates a functional map
$\C \in \R^{k_\M \times k_\N}$ from the target to the source with the
descriptor-preservation energy of \citet{nogneng2017informative}, refined with
spectral ICP and ZoomOut~\citep{melzi2019zoomout}; (iii) converts the regressor
to its functional form $\Rhat = \Rspat \Phi$ and transfers the skeleton as
\begin{equation}
  \J_\N = \Rhat \, \C \, \Psi^\dagger \X_\N .
  \label{eq:transfer}
\end{equation}
In this work we revisit steps (i) and (ii), which were solved with 1000
iterations of a first-order optimizer, and we add components that make the
transfer more robust to non-isometric targets, in particular clothed humans.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Exact spatial regressor}
\label{sec:regressor}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The regressor of \citet{musoni2021functional} minimizes
\begin{equation}
  E(\Rspat) = \omega_{rec} \|\Rspat\X_\M - \J_\M\|^2
            + \omega_{loc} \|\Rspat \odot \F - \Rspat\|^2
            + \omega_{spa} \| |\F - \one| \odot \Rspat\|^2
            + \omega_{con} \|\Rspat \one_{n_\M} - \one_Q\|^2 ,
  \label{eq:regressor_energy}
\end{equation}
where $\F \in \{0,1\}^{Q \times n_\M}$ is the locality mask: $F_{qi}=1$ iff
vertex $i$ has a skinning weight above $0.1$ for both joint $q$ and its parent.

\paragraph{Redundant terms.} Since $\F$ is binary,
$\Rspat\odot\F - \Rspat = -\Rspat \odot (\one - \F) = -|\F - \one| \odot \Rspat$:
the locality and sparsity terms coincide and act as a single penalty
$\omega \|\Rspat \odot (\one - \F)\|^2$ with $\omega = \omega_{loc} + \omega_{spa}$.
We use the weights of the released implementation, $\omega_{rec} = 1$,
$\omega = 1000 + 100$, $\omega_{con} = 10$.

\paragraph{Closed-form solution.} The energy is quadratic and decouples over
the rows of $\Rspat$. For joint $q$ with row $\mathbf{r} \in \R^{n_\M}$ we stack
the data terms as $\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{b}\|^2$ with
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{\omega_{rec}}\, \X_\M^\top \\ \sqrt{\omega_{con}}\, \one^\top \end{bmatrix} \in \R^{4 \times n_\M},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{\omega_{rec}}\, \J_{\M,q}^\top \\ \sqrt{\omega_{con}} \end{bmatrix} \in \R^{4},
\end{equation}
and split $\mathbf{r}$ into the coefficients inside ($S$) and outside ($O$) the
mask: $E_q = \|\mathbf{A}_S\mathbf{r}_S + \mathbf{A}_O\mathbf{r}_O - \mathbf{b}\|^2 + \omega \|\mathbf{r}_O\|^2$.
For fixed $\mathbf{r}_S$ the optimal $\mathbf{r}_O$ is a ridge regression which,
by the push-through identity, only requires a $4\times4$ inverse:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{r}_O^\star = \mathbf{A}_O^\top \left(\mathbf{A}_O\mathbf{A}_O^\top + \omega \mathbf{I}_4\right)^{-1} (\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{A}_S\mathbf{r}_S).
\end{equation}
Substituting it back gives the reduced energy
$E_q(\mathbf{r}_S) = (\mathbf{A}_S\mathbf{r}_S - \mathbf{b})^\top \mathbf{K} (\mathbf{A}_S\mathbf{r}_S - \mathbf{b})$
with $\mathbf{K} = \omega (\mathbf{A}_O\mathbf{A}_O^\top + \omega\mathbf{I}_4)^{-1} \succ 0$.
With $\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^\top$, the minimizers are the solutions of
the $4 \times |S|$ least-squares problem $\mathbf{L}^\top\mathbf{A}_S \mathbf{r}_S \approx \mathbf{L}^\top \mathbf{b}$.
Since $|S| \gg 4$ the minimizer is not unique, and we select the minimum-norm
one, $\mathbf{r}_S = (\mathbf{L}^\top\mathbf{A}_S)^\dagger \mathbf{L}^\top\mathbf{b}$,
which spreads the weight over all the vertices of the mask. The whole
regressor is obtained with $Q$ small dense solves, without iterations or
hyper-parameters of an optimizer.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Exact initial functional map}
\label{sec:fmap}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The initial $k \times k$ map ($k = 20$) minimizes
\begin{equation}
  E(\C) = \frac{\omega_d}{2} \|\C \widehat{\mathbf{D}}_\N - \widehat{\mathbf{D}}_\M\|^2
        + \omega_l \|\C \Lambda_\N - \Lambda_\M \C\|^2 ,
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{\mathbf{D}}$ are the spectral coefficients of the descriptors
(WKS~\citep{aubry2011wave} and wave kernel maps of the landmarks) and
$\Lambda$ the diagonal eigenvalue matrices. Because $\Lambda$ is diagonal,
$(\C\Lambda_\N - \Lambda_\M\C)_{ij} = C_{ij}(\lambda^\N_j - \lambda^\M_i)$ and
the energy decouples over the rows $\mathbf{c}_i$ of $\C$:
\begin{equation}
  \left(\omega_d \widehat{\mathbf{D}}_\N \widehat{\mathbf{D}}_\N^\top
        + 2\omega_l \operatorname{diag}\!\big((\bm{\lambda}^\N - \lambda^\M_i)^2\big)\right) \mathbf{c}_i
  = \omega_d \widehat{\mathbf{D}}_\N \, \widehat{\mathbf{d}}^{\M}_i ,
\end{equation}
with $\widehat{\mathbf{d}}^{\M}_i$ the $i$-th row of $\widehat{\mathbf{D}}_\M$.
This replaces the 1000 Adam iterations of the original implementation with $k$
linear systems of size $k \times k$.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Connected spectral bases for point clouds}
\label{sec:connectivity}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

For point clouds the Laplacian is built on a $k$-nearest-neighbour graph
\citep{sharp2020laplacian}. Loose garments create large gaps between surfaces
(e.g.\ between the hem of a skirt and the legs) that can split this graph into
several components. The multiplicity of the zero eigenvalue then equals the
number of components, the first eigenfunctions become indicator functions of
the components, and both the descriptors and the functional map lose their
meaning. We therefore check the connectivity of the graph and double the
number of neighbours (starting from 30, up to 240) until it is connected.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Skeleton priors on the target}
\label{sec:priors}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The transfer of Eq.~\eqref{eq:transfer} treats every joint independently. We
optionally correct the transferred joints $\J^0$ with two priors on the bone
lengths $\ell_b(\J) = \|\J_b - \J_{p(b)}\|$, where bone $b$ is identified by its
child joint:
\begin{equation}
  E(\J, s) = \omega_{d} \|\J - \J^0\|^2
           + \omega_{sym} \!\!\sum_{(a,b) \in \mathcal{P}}\!\! \big(\ell_a(\J) - \ell_b(\J)\big)^2
           + \omega_{prop} \sum_{b} \big(\ell_b(\J) - s\, \ell_b(\J_\M)\big)^2 .
  \label{eq:priors}
\end{equation}
The \emph{symmetry} term asks mirrored bones (left and right femur, \dots) to
have the same length; the set $\mathcal{P}$ of mirrored pairs is detected
automatically on the source, by reflecting its joints with respect to the
sagittal plane through the root and pairing mutual nearest neighbours whose
parents are themselves mirrored. On SMPL this recovers the expected nine
pairs. The \emph{proportion} term asks the bone lengths to match the source
up to a global scale $s$, optimized jointly with the joints. All residuals are
lengths, so the weights are directly comparable; we use
$\omega_d = \omega_{sym} = \omega_{prop} = 1$. The problem has $3Q + 1$ unknowns
and is solved with a trust-region nonlinear least-squares method.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Plausibility check}
\label{sec:plausibility}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The bone lengths also provide a test of the transfer that does not need any
ground truth. We compute the scale-normalized ratios
\begin{equation}
  \rho_b = \frac{\ell_b(\J_\N) / \ell_b(\J_\M)}{\sum_{b'} \ell_{b'}(\J_\N) \big/ \sum_{b'} \ell_{b'}(\J_\M)}
\end{equation}
and flag the transfer as implausible if some $\rho_b \notin [0.6, 1.6]$. A
failed correspondence typically maps whole joints onto the wrong limb, which
stretches or collapses some bones far beyond the natural variability of body
proportions (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp_failures}).

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Robust correspondence}
\label{sec:robust}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% code: commit 20fc426 (feature/target-skeleton-priors)

Large errors are failures of the correspondence, which no correction of the
skeleton can repair (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp_failures}). We make the correspondence
more robust with five complementary components.

\paragraph{Limb landmarks.} Besides the seven landmarks of
\citet{musoni2021functional} (head, hands, feet, front and back of the torso)
we optionally use four more: the front of the knees and the tip of the elbows.
They constrain the limbs, where loose garments hide the geometry the most.

\paragraph{Restarts.} The functional map is refined from a small initial map
of size $k=20$; a different initial size changes the basin reached by ICP and
ZoomOut. When the transferred skeleton is implausible
(Sec.~\ref{sec:plausibility}) we re-estimate the map from initial sizes
$k \in \{10, 30, 40\}$ and keep the result with the smallest
$\max_b |\log \rho_b|$, stopping as soon as one is plausible. Alternatively,
every candidate (the initial map and the three restarts) is refined with the
skeleton-guided refinement below, and the one with the highest final segment
consistency is kept (\emph{refined restarts}, Sec.~\ref{sec:exp_real}).
Often no candidate is right everywhere, while each limb is right in some
candidate. The \emph{per-chain restarts} therefore assemble the skeleton from
the refined candidates. The skeleton is split into its unbranched kinematic
chains, each starting at the root or at a child of a branching joint (for
SMPL the root, the legs, the spine, the neck and the arms from the collars).
The chain of the root is taken from the candidate kept by the refined
restarts, and every other chain from the candidate that ranks best on the sum
of two ranks: the segment consistency restricted to the source vertices of
the chain, and the agreement of the distances between the chain joints and
the target landmarks with the same distances on the source, after removing
the global scale. A chain placed on the wrong limb is far from the landmarks
of its own limb. The rule only uses the hierarchy of the skeleton and the
landmarks, so it applies to any skeleton.
% code: feature/target-skeleton-priors, commit d3f1581,
% restart_selection="chains", skeleton_transfer/diagnostics.py:kinematic_chains, chain_scores

\paragraph{Bijective ZoomOut.}
% code: feature/target-skeleton-priors, commit 27a49c0,
% skeleton_transfer/functional_map.py:bijective_zoomout
At every size, ZoomOut recovers the point-to-point map $T:\M\to\N$ by
matching each source vertex independently to its nearest neighbour in the
embedding $\Psi\C^\top$, so that many source vertices can collapse onto the
same region of the target. In the spirit of the discrete optimization
of \citet{ren2021discrete}, we refine together a map $T':\N\to\M$ in the
opposite direction, with its functional map $\C'$ (from source to target
coefficients), and recover each map from embeddings that also ask for
consistency with the other direction ($\C'\C \approx \mathbf{I}$):
\begin{align}
  T(i)  &= \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{j} \;\big\|\Phi_i - (\Psi\C^\top)_j\big\|^2 + w_b^2 \big\|(\Phi\C'^\top)_i - \Psi_j\big\|^2 , \\
  T'(j) &= \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{i} \;\big\|\Psi_j - (\Phi\C'^\top)_i\big\|^2 + w_b^2 \big\|(\Psi\C^\top)_j - \Phi_i\big\|^2 ,
\end{align}
with $w_b = 1$; both functional maps are then re-fitted to their
point-to-point maps at the next size. $T'$ starts from the map induced by
$\C^\top$ after ICP (the inverse of an orthogonal map). Only ZoomOut is
replaced: the skeleton-guided refinement below keeps one direction.

\paragraph{Landmark Voronoi cells.}
% code: feature/target-skeleton-priors, after cd70d3d (not yet committed),
% skeleton_transfer/descriptors.py:landmark_geodesics, voronoi_cells;
% FunctionalMapConfig.voronoi_steps = 5 (default), descriptors = "wave" | "geodesic" | "both"
The landmarks enter the initial map only through their wave kernel maps, and
their influence fades during ICP and ZoomOut. Their geodesic Voronoi cells
are a coarse segmentation of both shapes into corresponding parts (for the
seven landmarks: head, hands, feet, chest and back) that needs no learning,
no labels and no knowledge of the skeleton. We compute the shortest-path
distances $g_l(x)$ from every landmark $l$ along the edges of the mesh, or of
a $k$-nearest-neighbour graph for a point cloud ($k = 8$, doubled until the
graph is connected), and the cell indicators
$v_l(x) = [\,l = \arg\min_{l'} g_{l'}(x)\,]$. After ZoomOut we run five more
refinements at the final size $k = 120$ that append the indicators to the
spectral embeddings,
\begin{equation}
  T(i) = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{j} \;\big\|\Phi_i - (\Psi\C^\top)_j\big\|^2 + w_v^2 \|\mathbf{v}^\M(i) - \mathbf{v}^\N(j)\|^2 ,
\end{equation}
each followed by a re-fit of $\C$, with $w_v$ the median norm of the rows of
the spectral embedding (every $\mathbf{v}(x)$ has unit norm). Unlike the bone affinities of the
refinement below, the cells do not depend on the transferred skeleton, so
they also correct the map from which the first skeleton is computed, and the
map of every restart. We also tested the geodesic distances as descriptors
of the initial map, in place of or together with WKS and WKM: the distances
$g_l / \sqrt{A}$, with $A$ the area of the shape, and the Gaussians
$\exp\!\big(-(g_l / s\sqrt{A})^2\big)$ for $s \in \{0.1, 0.25, 0.5\}$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:exp_unified}).

\paragraph{Skeleton-guided refinement.} The transferred skeleton carries
extrinsic, pose-aware information that intrinsic descriptors lack: which
bone each point belongs to. For a point $x$ and a bone $b$ with segment
$[\J_{p(b)}, \J_b]$ we define the \emph{bone affinity}
\begin{equation}
  a_b(x) = \frac{\exp\!\big(-d_b(x)/\tau\big)}{\sum_{b'} \exp\!\big(-d_{b'}(x)/\tau\big)},
  \qquad d_b(x) = \operatorname{dist}\!\big(x, [\J_{p(b)}, \J_b]\big),
\end{equation}
with $\tau$ equal to $0.25$ times the mean bone length, so that $\mathbf{a}(x)$
is invariant to scale. On the source the affinities are computed with
$\J_\M$, on the target with the current transferred skeleton $\J_\N^{(t)}$
(after the priors of Sec.~\ref{sec:priors}). They are appended to the
spectral embeddings in the point-to-point step of ZoomOut at the final size
$k=120$,
\begin{equation}
  T(i) = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{j} \;\big\|\Phi_i - (\Psi\C^\top)_j\big\|^2 + w^2 \|\mathbf{a}^\M(i) - \mathbf{a}^\N(j)\|^2 ,
\end{equation}
with $w$ chosen so that the affinities have the median norm of the spectral
embedding; the functional map is then re-fitted to $T$, and the skeleton is
transferred again with Eq.~\eqref{eq:transfer}. Each iteration runs three
such map refinements; we iterate at most eight times, and stop earlier when
the segment consistency defined below has not improved by more than $0.002$
for two consecutive iterations (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp_robust}).

\paragraph{Unsupervised diagnostics.} To monitor the iterations without
ground truth we compute, for every intermediate result, the bone-ratio
deviation $\max_b |\log\rho_b|$, the length difference of mirrored bones, the
gap between the spectral and the pointwise transfer, the deviation of $\C$
from orthogonality, the coverage of the target by the point-to-point map, and
the \emph{segment consistency}: the fraction of source vertices mapped onto a
target point that is closest to the same bone, the target bones being placed
by the transferred skeleton. The latter measures the agreement between the map
and the skeleton, the quantity that the refinement improves.

\subsection{Loose clothing}
\label{sec:clothing}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% code: feature/target-skeleton-priors, commit cd70d3d, skeleton_transfer/clothing.py,
% TransferConfig.loose_clothing = LooseClothingConfig()

A skirt or a dress replaces the two legs with a single tube. The source
vertices of the thighs and knees have no counterpart on the target, and the
point-to-point map sends them onto the garment, far from the hidden legs; the
regressor of Eq.~\eqref{eq:transfer}, fitted on all the source vertices, then
pulls the knees onto the skirt and the hips downwards. We detect the source
vertices hidden under a loose garment and leave them out of the regressor.

The detection cannot use the skeleton, which is itself wrong under the
garment. It relies on a simpler observation: a loose garment is much flatter
than the body part it covers. For every point we estimate the radius of
curvature $r$ as the median, over the points within a distance $h$, of their
distance divided by the angle between the two normals (estimated by PCA over
the same neighbourhood); for a sphere of radius $r$ this gives $r$. The
neighbourhood is metric, $h = 0.006$ times the total bone length of the
source skeleton (about 26\,mm on SMPL), scaled on the target by the ratio of
the sizes of the two shapes. A fixed number of neighbours is not suitable: on
SMPL, whose mesh is much denser on the hands and the face, it measures the
curvature of the fingers and the nose, which a scan does not resolve, and the
hands and the head of every scan were taken for garments. A source vertex $i$
is covered if the score
\begin{equation}
  s(i) = \log \frac{r_\N\big(T(i)\big)}{\sigma\, r_\M(i)}
\end{equation}
exceeds $0.7$ (the target about twice as flat) after 30 iterations of
averaging over the source mesh, which keeps only large connected regions;
$\sigma$ is the ratio of the total bone lengths of the transferred and of the
source skeleton. At every evaluation of the skeleton (the initial map, every
restart and every iteration of the refinement) we recompute the covered set
and refit the regressor of Sec.~\ref{sec:regressor}, with the locality mask
restricted to the visible vertices. When a joint is covered almost entirely
(fewer than ten visible vertices in its mask), its mask falls back to the
visible vertices skinned to its own, its parent or its child bones, so that
the knee under a skirt is extrapolated from the visible shin and thigh. Since
the refinement uses the transferred skeleton, the corrected joints also guide
the following correspondences.

\subsection{Reproduction of the original method}
\label{sec:exp_parity}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% code: commit 4276de0 (refactor/package-structure), tests/test_parity.py

We re-implemented the method in NumPy/SciPy and compared it with the code
released with the original paper on its example (the template of the neutral
SMPL model\footnote{The example file of the original release,
\texttt{basicmodel\_m\_lbs.mat}, coincides field by field with the neutral
SMPL model v1.1.0, despite its name.} to a remeshed
Dynamic FAUST~\citep{bogo2017dfaust} scan). The Laplace--Beltrami spectra and
the locality mask coincide. Given the same regressor, our functional-map
pipeline transfers the same joints within 1.2\,mm, and the point-to-point maps
agree on 96\% of the vertices (the original uses an approximate
nearest-neighbour search). The full transfer takes about 1.7 minutes instead
of 3 on the same machine.

\paragraph{The original regressor had not converged.} After 1000 Adam
iterations the regressor of the original implementation leaves a residual
energy of $7.4\cdot10^{-3}$ and reconstructs the SMPL joints with errors up to
26\,mm, while the closed-form solution of Sec.~\ref{sec:regressor} reaches the
global minimum ($E \approx 10^{-29}$, exact joints). The two regressors
therefore produce different transfers: on the example the joints differ by up
to 5\,cm.

\paragraph{Pose invariance of the regressor.} The locality prior is meant to
make the regressor invariant to the pose. We measure it by posing the SMPL
template with linear blend skinning (random axis-angle rotations of standard
deviation $\sigma$ for every joint, 20 poses per value) and comparing the
regressed joints with the true ones obtained by forward kinematics
(Tab.~\ref{tab:pose_invariance}). The exact regressor reduces the mean error by
a factor of three to five.

\subsection{Synthetic benchmark with clothing}
\label{sec:exp_benchmark}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% code: commit 77fccbb (feature/target-skeleton-priors), tools/benchmark_synthetic.py

To evaluate the transfer against ground truth on non-isometric targets we
generate bodies with the neutral SMPL model~\citep{loper2015smpl}, with random shape
($\bm\beta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})$) and pose (axis-angle rotations with
standard deviation 0.3\,rad per joint, upright root), including the pose
corrective blend shapes. The true joints are given by the model. Each body is
sampled as a point cloud of 5000 vertices, so that it shares no connectivity
with the SMPL source, and receives the seven landmarks of
\citet{musoni2021functional}. Besides the nude bodies we simulate two loose
garments as geometric stress tests (Fig.~\ref{fig:synthetic}): a \emph{shirt},
obtained by displacing the torso and arms along the normals by
$4\,\mathrm{cm} \pm 50\%$ (smoothly varying), and a flared \emph{skirt}, that
replaces the legs from the hips to below the knees with a single surface
enclosing both. The skirt removes the separation between the legs, the main
cue used by intrinsic methods to tell them apart. The same eight bodies are
used for every condition; we report the mean Euclidean joint error.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/synthetic_targets.png}
  \caption{Synthetic targets with their ground-truth skeleton: the same SMPL
  body nude, with a loose shirt, with a skirt, and with both (front and side
  views).}
  \label{fig:synthetic}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Connectivity.} Without the check of Sec.~\ref{sec:connectivity} the
skirt splits the neighbourhood graph into two components (legs and body), and
the transfer collapses: on the first body the mean joint error is 832\,mm.
With the adaptive neighbourhood the same transfer has an error of 70\,mm.

\subsection{Skeleton priors and failure detection}
\label{sec:exp_failures}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Table~\ref{tab:priors} reports the error of the spectral transfer in the four
conditions. Loose clothing increases the error, and the combination of shirt
and skirt makes the transfer fail on most bodies.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{Mean joint error (mm) of the spectral transfer on the synthetic
  benchmark (8 bodies per condition). The last two rows only include the
  transfers that pass the plausibility check.}
  \label{tab:priors}
  \begin{tabular}{lcccc}
    \toprule
    & nude & shirt & skirt & shirt + skirt \\
    \midrule
    All transfers                          & 27.5 & 42.0 & 94.9 & 337.8 \\
    Flagged as implausible                 & 0/8  & 2/8  & 5/8  & 7/8   \\
    \midrule
    Plausible transfers                    & 27.5 & 33.8 & 59.5 & 76.5  \\
    Plausible transfers + priors (Eq.~\ref{eq:priors}) & 26.0 & 32.4 & 57.6 & 75.4 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Failure detection.} The plausibility check flags all the eight
transfers with an error above 10\,cm (up to 663\,mm), without using the ground
truth. It also flags six transfers with errors between 39 and 87\,mm, and the
largest error among the non-flagged transfers is 81\,mm. Inspecting the
failures shows that they are not left/right swaps (exchanging the labels of the
mirrored joints always increases the error) but partial collapses of the map,
where whole joints are transferred onto the wrong limb and some bones become
up to ten times longer than their true length.

\paragraph{Skeleton priors.} The priors consistently reduce the error of the
plausible transfers by about 5\% (Tab.~\ref{tab:priors}). The gain saturates for
$\omega_{sym}, \omega_{prop} \geq 1$: stronger weights do not help, and the
symmetry term alone does not help with clothing. The residual error is
therefore not explained by inconsistent bone lengths but by the placement of
the joints, and the large errors are failures of the correspondence that no
prior on the skeleton can repair. This motivates improving the correspondence
itself.

stronger than the sum of the parts: the skirt error drops from 118.8 to

reasonable starting skeleton: alone it cannot recover from a collapsed map,
while after the restarts and with the limb landmarks it removes most of the
residual error, and the skeleton priors stabilize it (the iterations stop
earlier, hence the shorter time). The gain concentrates on the joints hidden
by the garments: for the skirt the leg error goes from 206.7 to 87.2\,mm and
the spine error from 99.9 to 37.4\,mm. The loose shirt is the exception: the
mean error does not change and the median increases, the legs being placed
worse (from 45.4 to 64.1\,mm) while the arms improve (from 61.6 to 48.0\,mm).

covers them.

\paragraph{Convergence.} The refinement does not reach a fixed point: after
four iterations the largest joint displacement oscillates around 6\% of the
mean bone length instead of vanishing, while the error has already reached a
plateau (with the refinement alone, from 106.2\,mm before the first iteration
to 100.3\,mm after the fourth and 100.6\,mm after the eighth). A threshold on
the displacement of the joints is therefore not a usable stopping criterion.
Choosing, among the iterates, the one with the best diagnostic does not help
either: the iterations improve the result almost monotonically, and the last
iterate (48.8\,mm for the combined variant, before the priors) is better than
the one selected by any diagnostic (49.3\,mm at best, with the segment
consistency), the best possible choice being 46.5\,mm. The diagnostics are
instead effective as a stopping criterion: stopping when the segment
consistency has not improved by more than $0.002$ for two consecutive
iterations gives 49.4\,mm with 5.4 iterations on average instead of 7.7. We
adopt this rule in all the following experiments.

\label{sec:exp_real}

We evaluate on 4D-DRESS~\citep{wang2024dress}, which provides textured scans
of real people in their own clothes together with a registered SMPL body. We
use nineteen of its subjects (ten female, nine male) and one frame
per sequence, for a total of 185 scans: 149 in \emph{inner} garments only
(all nineteen subjects) and 36 with an \emph{outer} layer (jackets, coats;
subjects 00123, 00127, 00129, 00134 and 00179), which hides the body much
more. Within each sequence the frame is chosen by
farthest-point sampling on the SMPL pose parameters, so that the poses of the
benchmark are as varied as possible. The ground-truth joints are given by the
forward kinematics of the registered gender-specific SMPL model. Each scan is
sampled with 8000 points, after removing the points farther than 30\,cm from
the registered body (floor and capture artefacts), and the landmarks are the
scan points closest to the landmark vertices of the registration. The source
is the neutral SMPL template, as in the synthetic benchmark.
Table~\ref{tab:real} reports the results of the variants of
Sec.~\ref{sec:exp_robust}.

The real scans confirm the synthetic findings. The complete pipeline with
refined restarts reduces the mean error by 54\% with inner garments (from
86.5 to 40.0\,mm) and by 56\% with outer garments (from 108.6 to 47.5\,mm), and
the failures from 54 to 9 out of 185. No component alone is sufficient: the
limb landmarks give the largest single gain; only the combination of the three
components more than halves the failures, and the refined restarts reduce
them from 24 to 9.
The median, which ignores the failures, improves less (from 38.1 to 31.0\,mm
and from 46.5 to 37.0\,mm): the residual error of the successful transfers is
due to the garments themselves, which displace the surface by several
centimetres. The gain is largest on the legs (from 101.5 to 46.6\,mm with
inner and from 143.3 to 39.9\,mm with outer garments) and on the arms (from
92.3 to 40.0 and from 108.0 to 52.8\,mm), and smaller on the spine (from 56.7
to 31.1 and from 63.2 to 48.8\,mm). Unlike in the synthetic benchmark, the
least accurate joints are the ankles (50--61\,mm), covered by trousers and
shoes, and the hands and wrists (48--58\,mm), rather than the knees.

\paragraph{Bijective ZoomOut.} Unlike on the synthetic skirts, the bijective
ZoomOut helps on the real scans (last two rows of Tab.~\ref{tab:real}). With
the complete pipeline it reduces the mean error from 45.2 to 41.4\,mm and the
failures from 14 to 9 (six recovered, one new; Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the
185 paired transfers, $p = 0.001$); the error with outer garments slightly
increases (from 46.7 to 47.5\,mm). With the baseline it reduces the mean error
from 95.0 to 90.8\,mm and improves 140 of the 185 transfers ($p < 10^{-9}$),
at every level of error.

The difficulty varies considerably across subjects. The baseline fails on six
of the seven scans of one subject (mean 152.1\,mm) and on five of the nine of
another (198.1\,mm), which the complete pipeline brings to 42.8\,mm without
failures and to 55.6\,mm with one failure; the nine remaining failures are
spread over eight subjects. The pipeline is similarly accurate on female
and male subjects (39.2 and 43.8\,mm), as is the baseline (95.6 and
85.9\,mm). The benchmark grew in three steps: 25 scans of two subjects
(middle frame of each sequence), 88 scans of ten subjects, and the 185 scans
of Tab.~\ref{tab:real}. The 97 scans of the nine subjects added last are
harder than the first 88 (45.1 against 37.5\,mm with the complete pipeline,
106.6 against 73.3\,mm with the baseline): the conclusions of the comparison,
however, did not change.

\paragraph{Diagnostics and failures.} Over the 6657 intermediate results the
diagnostics are close to each other: all but the spectral--pointwise gap are
about equally correlated with the error (Spearman $\rho$ from 0.82 for the
bone ratios and the coverage to 0.85 for the orthogonality, 0.73 for the gap),
and the symmetry, the orthogonality, the coverage and the segment consistency
detect the failures equally well (AUC 0.92, against 0.91 for the bone ratios
and 0.88 for the gap). The plausibility
check is reliable for the variants without the guided refinement (it flags
44 of the 47 failures with restarts, with ten false alarms), but less so
after the refinement, which can produce a skeleton with plausible
proportions on a wrong correspondence (4 of the 9 final failures are
flagged). With the stopping rule the refinement uses 4.8 iterations on
average.

\paragraph{Selecting the restart.} Choosing the restart with the segment
consistency instead of the bone ratios, before the refinement, does not help
consistently: on the targets where the restarts are triggered it reduces the
error before the refinement on the first 25 scans of 4D-DRESS (from 99.2 to
73.0\,mm) but increases it on the synthetic benchmark (from 95.5 to
101.4\,mm, four failures instead of two), and no other diagnostic or
combination does better than the bone ratios on both. Before the refinement a
collapsed map and the skeleton it induces can be mutually consistent. After
the refinement the diagnostics are reliable (Tab.~\ref{tab:diagnostics}), so
we refine every candidate and keep the one with the highest final segment
consistency. We selected this rule on the 9 synthetic and 4 real targets of
the first benchmark where the restarts are triggered, where it reduces the
final error from 73.7 to 70.3\,mm and from 87.5 to 66.1\,mm. On the 185 scans
of Tab.~\ref{tab:real}, which include 160 scans not used for this choice, the
restarts are triggered on 65 scans; on these the refined restarts reduce the
mean error from 101.3 to 62.0\,mm and the failures from 24 to 9, improving 36
of the 65 transfers and worsening 5 (by more than 1\,mm). An oracle choosing
the best candidate would reach 54.2\,mm with 6 failures, so the choice of the
candidate, not
the candidates themselves, is the main source of the remaining failures. The
cost is one refinement per candidate, i.e.\ about four times the time of the
complete pipeline on the triggered targets only.

\paragraph{Per-chain restarts.} No other whole-candidate rule closes this gap.
On the candidates of the 65 triggered scans we tested, besides the
diagnostics above, the round-trip error of the map induced by $\C$, the
fraction of torn source edges, the depth of the joints under the surface
compared with the source, and joint-wise fusions of the candidates (median,
medoid): none is better than the segment consistency (63.9\,mm and 10
failures before the priors; the best combination of two or three scores,
chosen among 220, gives 59.1\,mm with the same number of failures, a gain we
attribute to the selection itself). Choosing each chain separately has a
much larger margin: an oracle choosing the best candidate per chain reaches
45.0\,mm with 4 failures, against 54.2\,mm with 6 for the whole-candidate
oracle. The landmark agreement is the per-chain signal most correlated with
the error of the chain (Spearman $\rho = 0.57$ among the candidates of a
chain, 0.54 for the chain segment consistency). We selected the per-chain
rule among six signals and a few of their combinations on these 65 scans,
and validated it on 48 new synthetic bodies (twelve per condition), where the
restarts are triggered on 19: there the per-chain choice reduces the error
before the priors from 77.7 to 63.5\,mm and the failures from 5 to 1.
In the complete runs (Tab.~\ref{tab:real}) the per-chain restarts reduce the
mean error from 41.4 to 38.8\,mm (median from 32.2 to 31.6\,mm) and the
failures from 9 to 7 (three recovered, one new). They improve 35 transfers
and worsen 16 ($p < 0.001$ on the 57 transfers that change), and on the 65
triggered scans they reach 54.4\,mm, as the whole-candidate oracle. The gain
is largest with outer garments (from 47.5 to 40.7\,mm); 12 of the 19 subjects
improve and 3 get worse. On the synthetic benchmark (six bodies per
condition) the mean error goes from 49.8 to 45.8\,mm and the failures from 2
to 1, mostly with shirt and skirt (from 88.9 to 76.1\,mm), with no change on
the nude bodies. The other statistics of this section refer to the refined
restarts.

\paragraph{Loose clothing.} On the real scans the detection of
Sec.~\ref{sec:clothing} leaves out few source vertices (median 1.4\%, 4.8\%
at the 90th percentile, 15\% at most). On the
185 scans it is neutral (last row of Tab.~\ref{tab:real} before the plain
ZoomOut): the mean error goes from 38.8 to 38.5\,mm, 24 transfers improve and
21 get worse by more than 1\,mm ($p = 0.67$), and one transfer becomes a
failure. The changes concentrate on the scans where something is detected:
only 2 of the 108 scans with less than 2\% of covered vertices change by more
than 5\,mm. With a fixed number of neighbours instead of the metric
neighbourhood, the detection left out 22--41\% of the source vertices of
every scan, mostly the hands and the head (Sec.~\ref{sec:clothing}), with the
same mean error. The real scans with skirts and dresses of
Sec.~\ref{sec:exp_unified} do not show a gain either.

\paragraph{Comparison with SMPL fitting.}
% code: skeleton_transfer/smpl_fitting.py, tools/fit_smpl_baseline.py (feature/target-skeleton-priors 05187bc)
When the template is SMPL, the direct alternative is to register the
parametric body to the scan and read the joints of the fit. We implemented a
standard optimization-based registration for clothed scans with the same
inputs as the transfer (the same 8000 points and 11 landmarks, the neutral
model): a rigid alignment on the landmarks of the torso and legs, a pose fit
to the landmarks, and an ICP-style fit of pose, shape and translation with a
robust (Geman--McClure) scan-to-body term, a body-to-scan term that is
stronger where the body leaves the scan, and L2 priors on pose and shape. On
an unclothed posed SMPL body it recovers the joints within 6\,mm. On the 185
scans it reaches a mean (median) error of 17.5 (15.6)\,mm, 17.2\,mm with inner
and 18.7\,mm with outer garments, with one failure, against 41.4 (32.2)\,mm and
9 failures of the complete transfer with refined restarts, which is more
accurate on only four of the 185 scans (38.8 (31.6)\,mm, 7 failures and five
scans with the per-chain restarts). The fit takes 62\,s per scan on the CPU; the transfer took
about 400\,s on the CPU (measured on the first 88 scans, on an otherwise idle
machine, before the bijective ZoomOut) and about 60\,s with the
nearest-neighbour searches of the maps on a GPU, which give the same maps. This
setting favours the fit by construction: the ground truth is itself a SMPL
registration, so the fitted model, its joint regressor and its kinematic tree
are those of the ground truth. The comparison shows, however, that on humans
with a SMPL template the transfer is not competitive with a parametric fit,
and that its use is for templates and skeletons without a parametric model.

\paragraph{Complete pipeline and loose clothing.} Table~\ref{tab:unified}
confirms the findings of Sec.~\ref{sec:exp_real}, mostly on datasets that
were not used to design the method: the complete pipeline halves the mean
error (from 82.8 to 40.7\,mm) and divides the failures by four (from 223 to
58). It improves every group, but the animals only a little (from 111.7 to
104.7\,mm). The handling of loose clothing gives a small gain overall (to
39.9\,mm, 50 failures), mostly on the children (from 69.1 to 61.3\,mm), and
is neutral on the 36 real skirts and dresses of CloSe-Di (from 39.8 to
40.1\,mm, one failure fewer), where the complete pipeline is already as
accurate as with inner garments. We keep it on by default, since no group
gets worse by more than 0.3\,mm.

\paragraph{Ingredients of the functional map.} Most of the remaining
failures are failures of the initial functional map. To find which
ingredient limits it we ran a study of the map alone (initial map, ICP and
bijective ZoomOut with all the landmarks enforced, no restarts, refinement
or priors) on 57 samples of the development set, evenly spaced within
fixed numbers per group that favour the hard groups (18 tight humans, 29
clothed, 4 children, 6 animals), measuring the error of the spectral
transfer (Tab.~\ref{tab:mapstudy}). The size of the map has little effect.
A final size of 150 or 200 instead of 120 changes the mean error by less
than 3\,mm. An initial size of 30 instead of 20 lowers it (to 74.9\,mm) but
improves and worsens as many samples (23 each), and an initial size of 10
is clearly worse (108.3\,mm); this variability is what the restarts exploit.
Other bases do not help either: the Coordinates Manifold
Harmonics~\todo{cite CMH}, which represent the coordinates exactly, give
about the same mean error (83.1\,mm), while the Laplacian
of the source point cloud (instead of its mesh) and the scale-invariant
Laplacian~\citep{aflalo2013scale}, with the area element scaled by the
Gaussian curvature ($|K|^{1/2}$ in Tab.~\ref{tab:mapstudy}; $|K|$ is worse
still), are worse. The descriptors matter more. The WKS alone fails on almost every
sample (342.0\,mm): all the information that makes the map work comes from the landmarks. The
wave kernel maps alone, the heat kernel signature and maps~\citep{sun2009concise}
and above all the geodesic distances from the landmarks are better than the
WKS/WKM pair (from 83.9 to 75.3, 61.9 and 55.1\,mm). Segmentation
information is the strongest cue, provided it enters the point-to-point
steps rather than the initial map: the ground-truth segmentation into the 24
SMPL parts, transferred from the registration, reduces the error to 32.1\,mm
when appended to the spectral embeddings after ZoomOut, improving 51 of the 57
samples and worsening none, but only to 69.2\,mm when used as descriptors of
the initial map. The landmark Voronoi cells of Sec.~\ref{sec:robust} are the
unsupervised counterpart of this oracle and recover about two thirds of its
gain (48.3\,mm, 35 samples improved and 6 worsened by more than 2\,mm), while
as descriptors they give 67.1\,mm.

\paragraph{Voronoi cells in the complete pipeline.} Added to the complete
pipeline (last two columns of Tab.~\ref{tab:unified}), the Voronoi cells
reduce the mean error from 39.9 to 34.4\,mm and the failures from 50 to 13:
all the 37 failures they change are recovered and none is introduced, 289
transfers improve and 138 get worse by more than 1\,mm (16 by more than
10\,mm, none by more than 30\,mm; Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the 890 paired
transfers, $p < 10^{-20}$). Every group improves. The largest gains are on
BUFF (from 64.5 to 49.2\,mm, from 11 failures to none), on the children (from
61.3 to 40.3\,mm, from 4 failures to none) and on the animals (from 101.2 to
73.8\,mm). The remaining failures are five scans of humans and the eight
hippopotamids, whose massive bodies and short legs defeat every variant (their
error goes from 297 to 183\,mm). Using the geodesic distances also as
descriptors of the initial map lowers the mean error a little further (to
33.5\,mm), but less reliably: four transfers of BUFF fail, and three
transfers get worse by more than 30\,mm. We therefore make the Voronoi cells
with the WKS/WKM descriptors the default of the pipeline.

\paragraph{Children and animals.} The transfer is not specific to the
human template: with the SMAL template the error on felids, canids, equids
and bovids is 33--46\,mm (22--26\% of the mean bone length), comparable in
relative terms with the real clothed humans (16--30\%). Small children remain the
hardest case relative to their size: the error on the children is 54\% of
the mean bone length, and 121\% for the infants (factor 1, 64.4\,mm for bones
of 53\,mm), against 9.6\% for the synthetic adults. Besides the different
proportions of an infant, which the map from an adult template has to absorb,
the proportion prior of Sec.~\ref{sec:priors} and the plausibility check
assume the proportions of the source, which do not hold for an infant; a
source closer to the target (a child template) would remove both
mismatches.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The ground truth is the joints of a registered
parametric body, which has its own error under loose clothing; on BUFF it is
a pose fitted by us. The real scans with skirts and dresses are still few
(36 in the development set), and CAPE~\citep{ma2020cape} remains to be
added. The results on the held-out test set will be reported with the final
configuration.
